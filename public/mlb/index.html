Does a bigger payroll buy more wins?

This started as a USC team project, where I did most of the data work and modeling. The class model looked strong. When I went back to it on my own, I found its biggest effect came from a vote cast after the season, and payroll was in dollars that meant different things in 2000 and 2024. So I rebuilt it.

The question

Can a team buy wins?

Every front office works with a budget. If spending more really buys wins, the next question is what one extra win costs. That's the number I wanted.

The data: 720 team-seasons, every MLB team from 2000 to 2024 (2020 isn't in it). For each one: payroll, wins, home attendance, how many different players the team used, average age, and Manager of the Year votes.

Step 1 of 3

Start with the class model

First look

Payroll against wins, one dot per team-season. There's a slope, but a loose one: payroll alone explains about 9% of the differences in wins (R² 0.09). Plenty of expensive teams still lose, and some cheap ones win.

Checking the rows first

Before trusting any model, I checked the data. The class slides said 2000–2025, but the data actually ends in 2024. And the 2022 Washington Nationals appeared twice, with different payroll and wins.

Their real record was 55–107, so I dropped the 71-win row. That left 720 team-seasons.

The class model

In class, our team added four variables: home attendance, roster churn (how many different players a team used), average age, and a manager score built from Manager of the Year votes. R² jumped to 0.48, payroll was significant, and the model put one extra win at about $21.3M.

On a slide, that looks like a finished answer.

What bothered me

The biggest effect in the model was the manager score. But Manager of the Year votes are cast after the season, and voters mostly reward teams that won. So the arrow partly runs backwards: winning produces the votes.

Attendance has the same problem: fans show up when a team is winning. A front office can't use either one to plan a season, because both only show up once the wins do.

Step 2 of 3

Put every season's dollars on the same scale

Dollars from different years

Payroll was in raw dollars, but the average MLB payroll went from $55.7M in 2000 to $153.7M in 2024. A $100M payroll meant very different things:

$100M in 2000 = 1.8× league average$100M in 2024 = 0.65× league average

So I measured each team's payroll against the league average that same season. 1.5 means 50% more than the average team that year.

Same teams, fair scale

On that scale, payroll alone explains more: R² goes from 0.09 to 0.15. Money matters more than the raw dollars showed.

Then payroll disappeared

Next I put the adjusted payroll back into the class model. Its effect dropped to almost exactly zero (p = 0.98). For a business that spends over $100M a year on players, that didn't add up, so I went looking for what was taking its place.

It was attendance. Adjusted payroll and attendance move together (correlation 0.75): big-market teams both spend more and draw bigger crowds. And attendance is partly caused by winning. So the model was handing payroll's credit to a variable that's partly the result itself.

A model can look strong because the result is explaining itself.

Step 3 of 3

Keep only what isn't driven by the result

The final model

I dropped the manager score and attendance and kept adjusted payroll, roster churn and average age. R² fell from 0.48 to 0.27. That's lower, but I'd rather present a 27% I can defend than a 48% that's partly the result explaining itself.

Per one standard deviation: roster churn −3.1 wins, payroll +2.9, average age +2.9. Money matters, but in this data a stable roster is linked to about as many wins.

How much is a win?

Every +10% of league-average payroll goes with about +0.72 wins (95% CI 0.48 to 0.96). Flip it around: one extra win takes about 14% of a league-average payroll.

One extra win costs about $21.3M.

+10% of league-average payroll → +0.72 wins · $153.7M ÷ 7.21 wins ≈ $21.3M per win, 2024 dollars

The answer

Yes, money buys wins, at about $21.3M per extra win in 2024 dollars.

It's the same $21.3M the class model gave, for a different reason. That one was in raw dollars mixed across 24 seasons, from a model with after-the-fact variables. This one is in 2024 dollars, from a model I can defend.

If I were advising a front office, I'd add one thing: before paying about $21M for a win, look at roster stability. Teams that cycle through more players win less, though part of that is injuries and mid-season sell-offs.

What this can't tell you

  • It's observational data, so this is association, not a clean causal effect.
  • Reverse causality can run through payroll too: winning raises revenue, which funds payroll.
  • Roster churn partly reflects injuries and mid-season sell-offs, which losing can cause.
  • The final model explains 27% of the differences in wins. Most of what decides a season isn't in here.
  • No team fixed effects, so stable traits like market size and ownership are only partly controlled.

How much capacity should TSMC add next year?

TSMC is Taiwan's pride, and I wanted to understand why it's so good. I come from business analytics, so I picked the part of the story I could actually work on with numbers: how much should TSMC build to keep up with the orders coming in?

The question

Build too few, or build too many

Every year TSMC has to decide how much capacity to add. Build too few, and demand shows up with no capacity to meet it; that revenue is just gone. Build too many, and machines sit idle while depreciation keeps running.

So the whole project comes down to one question: how much capacity should TSMC add next year? To answer it I needed four things: how full the fabs are today, how much utilization moves gross margin, what next year's demand could look like, and which mistake costs more, a shortage or idle machines.

Step 1 of 4

How full are the fabs right now?

Why start here

Utilization is what you actually produce divided by the most you could produce. If a lot of machines are sitting idle, a bit more demand next year is easy to absorb, so there's no rush to build. If the fabs are already full, any rise in demand turns into a shortage.

The problem: TSMC stopped publishing utilization after 2020. The most important years, the AI years, are exactly the ones we can't see.

So I rebuilt it

Utilization = shipments ÷ capacity. Shipments are in every quarterly report. For the last four quarters (2025Q4–2026Q3):

16.95 M wafers shipped ÷ 18.16 M capacity = 93%

Capacity is the hard part. Only the annual report (the 20-F) gives one number for the whole year. So I spread each year into quarters, assuming capacity grows in a straight line from one year to the next.

A range, not a point

That yearly number is also rounded. "Approximately 17 million" could be anything from 16.5 to 17.5 million. So I ran a low and a high version of capacity, and utilization becomes a band instead of a single line.

Check 1 · an answer key

From 2009 to 2012, TSMC still published capacity every quarter. I used those 16 quarters as an answer key: I gave my method only the yearly number and compared its quarters with the real ones.

On average it was off by 2.01%. The worst miss was 5.35%, in 2009, during the financial crisis: TSMC paused expansion, but my straight line assumed capacity was still climbing. So the method is least accurate at turning points.

Check 2 · a test I failed

TSMC's quarterly reports say in words whether utilization went up or down. I compared my direction with theirs over 40 quarters, and set the bar at 80% before running it.

I got 72.5%. It didn't pass, and I report it that way. It's tempting to see a result and then go back and change the test. I think you set the bar first, run it, and live with the result.

What it shows

2021–22: about 100%, fully booked. Then from 2023Q2 to 2025Q1, eight straight quarters under 80%, bottoming around 72%. That's deeper than the 2019 trough, which TSMC reported at 81% for the year. The last four quarters are back to 93%.

Next question

Now I know how full the fabs are. But how much does that matter for profit? If utilization barely moves margin, building a little less wouldn't hurt. So the next question: for every point of utilization, how many points of gross margin?

Step 2 of 4

How much does utilization move gross margin?

The obvious chart

My first move was to line up utilization and gross margin for every quarter since 2012. They barely track each other: correlation is only 0.17 (R² 0.03). In 2023–24, utilization dropped to about 72%, yet margin stayed above most earlier years.

If I stopped there, I'd conclude utilization doesn't matter for profit. I didn't buy it. A fab is mostly fixed cost, so running it emptier should hurt margin. When the data disagrees with how the business works, I first check whether I'm comparing the wrong things. Here I was: pricing, process mix and depreciation in 2014 look nothing like 2024, so each period sits at its own margin level, and mixing them hides the relationship.

Split by era

So I split the quarters into three eras: before 2021, the 2021–22 shortage, and the AI era from 2023. Each era keeps its own margin level, and I only ask how margin moves within an era. Inside each one, the relationship is clear: correlation of 0.67 to 0.80. Put into one regression:

+10 pts utilization ≈ +3.3 pts gross margin  (R² 0.78)

The gap between eras turned out to be the second finding. Since 2023, margin runs about 12.6 pts higher at the same utilization. The fabs aren't busier than before; each wafer earns more. To me that says AI raised price, not volume.

For the capacity question, this tells me idle machines have a real cost. Next I needed to know how likely they are, and that depends on demand.

Step 3 of 4

What could next year's demand look like?

Demand as a die

I didn't want to bet on a single demand forecast. One number tells you nothing about how wrong it could be, and for capacity, being wrong is the whole risk.

So I took TSMC's actual shipment growth for the last 13 years (2013–2025) and treated each year as one face of a die. Using real years keeps the bad ones in, like 2023 at −21%, instead of smoothing them into a bell curve. Then I rolled it 10,000 times. Each roll is one possible next year, and every capacity choice gets tested against all of them.

Two risks that cross

As I add more capacity, the chance of a shortage falls and the chance of idle machines (utilization under 80%) rises. The two are equal at about +9.5%.

But equal odds don't mean equal cost. Running short and sitting idle hurt the business by very different amounts, so I had to put a price on each.

Step 4 of 4

Which mistake costs more: a shortage or idle machines?

Price each mistake
A wafer that can't be shipped: ~NT$192K lost  ·  A wafer of capacity idle for a year: ~NT$41K

Per wafer, a shortage costs about 5× as much as idle capacity.

Then I ran both costs through all 10,000 simulated years. The cheapest add is about +7%, and the bottom is flat from +5% to +9.5%: anywhere in that range is within NT$1–7B of the best, under 0.2%. That flat bottom matters more to me than the exact +7%. It means there's room to choose inside the range without a real penalty.

I also tested a harsher assumption for idle cost (right panel). There, every add costs more than it saves, and the answer drops toward 0%. So the recommendation depends on how idle capacity is priced, and that's a number I'd want from TSMC itself.

My answer

Add +5% to +9.5% capacity next year, centered around +7%.

What would change it most isn't demand; it's my capacity estimate. Moving it within its range shifts the answer from +2% to +12%. So if I were inside TSMC, the first thing I'd ask for is the real capacity number, by process node, including advanced packaging.

What didn't work, and what's missing

  • The direction test failed: 72.5% against a bar of 80% that I set before running it. Quarter-to-quarter changes under 5% are noise.
  • Utilization after 2020 is an estimate, and it's least accurate at turning points.
  • Front-end wafers only. CoWoS packaging, the real bottleneck in 2023, isn't in the model.
  • No price, exchange rate or process mix in the model, and no customers walking away during a shortage.
